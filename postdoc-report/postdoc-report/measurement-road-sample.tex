\chapter{Spectral BRDF Measurement of road samples}


The design and operation of a transport system require careful consideration of numerous things, such as the installation of public lighting, minimizing light pollution, temperature control, and ensuring the visibility of road markings.
Central to addressing these considerations is to know the reflectance of the road surface, which necessitates simulations or measurements.
However, conducting these simulations or measurements is often costly due to the time requirements and the need for specialized equipment.
A key question arises: Can the reflectance of a road surface be predicted if the formulation of the surface and the reflectance of its individual components are known?
The relationship between solar albedo and BRDF provides a pathway to answering this question.
Equations \eqref{eq_white_sky_albedo} and \eqref{eq_black_sky_albedo} illustrate how solar albedo can be derived from BRDF.
This indicates that if the BRDF model can predict the BRDF for any given direction of incidence and observation, then it can also predict the solar albedo of the surface.
Chapter~\ref{ch: brdf-models} provides several BRDF models expressed as functions of incident and viewing angles.
However, these models often require additional parameters that depend on the surface's characteristics.
For instance, the ON model requires knowledge of the diffuse albedo ($\rho$) and surface roughness ($\sigma$).
For each single-material component, we can calibrate a reflectance model by fitting it to spectral BRDF measurements and extracting the relevant parameters.
Once BRDF models are established for each individual component, they can be combined to construct a hybrid BRDF model for the multi-material pavement.
This model can then be fitted to the BRDF measurements to determine the weighting factors associated with each component.
Accordingly, the BRDF of this pavement can be predicted or any direction of incidence and observation.
However, achieving this lies in obtaining accurate and reliable spectral BRDF measurements, which serve as essential input to fitting process.

To support this, this chapter first introduces 2 formulations of road samples and their constituent components.
Then it provides a spectral BRDF measurement method using the equipments developed by Cerema to measure their BRDF.
This approach is validated first through through BRDF measurements on Spectralon (a material with well-known Lambertian reflection model and a given diffuse albedo), and then comparison with results obtained from Université Gustave Eiffel (UGE).
The measured spectral BRDF results for both the composite (mix-material) and individual (single-material) samples are presented and analyzed in the last section.

\section{Road samples}

This section first presents 4 different formulations of road samples, along with their individual constituent materials, as provided by a French company.
In order to ensure reliable BRDF measurements for each single-component sample, it is required that the surface to be measured must be flat.
For this purpose, this section then describes how to construct such samples.

\subsection{Two Road formula}

In this study, we chose 2 different formulations of road samples (provided by a French company - Spie Batignolles), along with their individual constituent materials (seen in Table~\ref{tab:2-formulations}).
In order to ensure reliable BRDF measurements for each single-component sample, it requires that the surface to be measured must be flat, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:F2-F3}.
Each formulation incorporates limestone filler, a very fine powder present in small proportions.
As such, the impact of limestone filler has been disregarded in the analysis.
Formulations F2 and F3 share similar component structures.
The main difference between them is in the binder: F2 uses bitumen, while F3 employs a pigmented synthetic binder $TiO_2$.
Across all single-component samples, the materials can be broadly classified into two categories: binders and aggregates.
The binders include bitumen and the synthetic binder.
The aggregates comprise two main types:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Sorrèze limestone (0/2 mm),
    \item Lazard rock with two different size regions (2/6.3 mm and 6.3/10 mm).
\end{itemize}
For clarity, these two types Lazard are hereafter referred to as Lazard 2/6 and Lazard 6/10, respectively.
It is difficult to perform direct measurements on binders, as creep occurs and the surface does remain perfectly planar.
So we combine the Sorrèze limestone with bitumen and synthetic binder respectively.
As such, we have two different mastics corresponding to F2 and F3 provided by the same French company (Spie Batignolles), as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:F2-F3}.

\subsection{Construction of single-material samples}

The procedure for fabricating a one-component sample with a flat surface is as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Prepare all the materials and tools including:
          \begin{itemize}
              \item 3 liquid products (as illuminated in Fig~\ref{fig:3-materials}): Renlease QZ511, REN HY956 from RenShape and Resin - RENLAM MS-1
              \item Aluminum mould, as seen in Fig~\ref{fig:mould-empty}
              \item Brush
              \item Stainless steel spatula
              \item Mixing container
              \item Clean aggregates
              \item Fontainebleau sands
          \end{itemize}

    \item Clean the mould, then install the aluminum mould and secure it with 2 screws.
    \item Coating the mould with unmold liquid Renlease QZ511 using the brush, as seen in Fig~\ref{fig:module-oil}.
    \item Put a single layer of aggregates, making sure they are as close together as possible, as seen in Fig~\ref{fig:mould-rock}.
    \item Place a layer of sand on top of the aggregate to prevent the resin from passing through the pores between the aggregates, as shown in Fig~\ref{fig:module-rock-sand}.
    \item Put $300g$ of RENLAM MS-1 resin and $100g$ of REN HY956 hardener in the mixing container, and mix them together, then put in $800g$ of Fontainebleau sand, as seen in Fig~\ref{fig:mix-3-material}.
    \item Mix the sands with the liquids in the container until homogenous, then leave them (see Fig~\ref{fig:mix-3-material-after}) for about 1 hour to warm up.
    \item Pour the homogenous glue into the mould until it is full, then make sure the top surface is flat and leave to rest for 8 hours, as seen in Fig~\ref{fig:mould-3-material}
    \item Unmould this mould and remove the sand from the surface of the aggregates, as shown in Fig~\ref{fig:sample-final}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Measurement set-up}


The set-up developed by Cerema~\cite{2023_Liandrat, 2023_Muzet} was initially designed for measuring photometric BRDF in accordance with International Commission on Illumination (CIE) recommendations, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:gonio-reflectometer}.
It consists of 3 elements: light source, the sample holder and the camera.
A halogen lamp with a broadband spectral emission ranging from $350nm$ to $2500nm$ is employed as the illumination source, and its spectrum is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:irrad_halogene_cerema}.
It is mounted on a circular metal arc with a radius of $2.3 m$, allowing the sample to be illuminated at a constant distance from various zenith angles $\gamma$, ranging from vertical ($\gamma=0^\circ$) to grazing angles.
The detection system comprises a camera calibrated in photometric luminance and mounted on a second circular metal arc with a radius of $1.9 m$, which allows the selection of any observation zenith angle ($\alpha$).
This camera is attached to the end of a rotating metal arm ($1.8m$) and oriented toward the sample positioned along the arm’s axis, ensuring that the surface is always observed from the same viewpoint.
By rotating this arm, the azimuthal angle ($\beta$) between the illumination and observation planes can be varied.
The sample holder is positioned at another side of this rotating arm, and can be rotated around it to modify the azimuth angle $\delta$ between the observation plane and the axis of the sample (x axis).
The joint movement of these 3 elements is performed automatically by a computer, which allows the light reflected by the sample surface to be measured under any lighting and observation configuration.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=1\linewidth]{./figures/spectral-brdf-measurements/fig_irradiance_halogene.png}
    \caption{Spectrum of halogene lamp (Cerema)}
    \label{fig:irrad_halogene_cerema}
\end{figure}

As known, the camera is typically used to capture the photometric luminance.
If we want to measure the spectral BRDF of roads, we need a sensor to be able to detect the spectral radiance, which is spectroradiometer.
As seen in Figure~\ref{fig:gonio-spectro}, the new detection systems is placed at the same rotating arm, and consists of the lens, the lens support, the spectroradiometer, and the optical cable (used to connect the lens and the spectroradiometer).
The camera in the original set-up is replaced by a lens connected with the spectroradiometer through a optical cable.
Of course, to adjust the observation zenith angle is less flexible compared to the original set-up because we need to manually adjust the height of the lens support.
Except this, the rest is still controlled by the computer.

\subsection{Methodology}

To have a better understanding of the measurement method, the simplified set-up is as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:gonio-spectro}.
We have the main configuration parameters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\gamma$: the incident zenith angle (the angle between the normal and the illumination plane);
    \item $\alpha$: the observation zenith angle (the angle between the normal and the observation plane);
    \item $\beta$: the azimuth angle between the illumination and observation planes;
    \item $\delta$: the azimuth angle between the observation plane and the axis of the sample (x axis), not marked in the Figure~\ref{fig:gonio-spectro};
    \item $\omega$: the Field Of View (FOV) of lens;
    \item $l$: the distance between the lens center and the sample center, limited to the length of the rotating arm ($<1.8m$);
    \item $H$: the height of the lens center relative to the horizontal plane of the sample surface, depending on $l$ and $\alpha$;
    \item $D$: the diameter of light spot on the sample surface emitted from the lens center, depending on $\omega$, $l$ and $\alpha$.
\end{itemize}
Since the angles $\gamma, \beta, \delta$ can be adjusted automatically by the computer and not limited to measurement environment, so we could choose any values.
However, the parameters $l$, $D$ and $H$ are constrained to the length of the rotating arm, the size of the sample, and the height of the measurement room, respectively.
Following detailed evaluation, $l = 60\mathrm{cm}$ was adopted, and the observation angles ($\alpha$) were varied from $15^\circ$ to $55^\circ$ in increments of $5^\circ$.
The resulting parameters $H$ and $D$, provided in Table~\ref{tab:configuration_parameters}, are within reasonable limits.
To ensure sufficient measurements configurations, the chosen CIE angles configurations are summarized as below:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\alpha$: ranging from $15^\circ$ to $55^\circ$ in increments of $5^\circ$;
    \item $\beta$: $0^\circ, 10^\circ, 30^\circ, 50^\circ, 70^\circ, 90^\circ, 110^\circ, 130^\circ, 150^\circ, 160^\circ, 165^\circ, 170^\circ$;
    \item $\gamma$: $0^\circ, 10^\circ, 15^\circ, 20^\circ, 25^\circ, 30^\circ, 35^\circ, 40^\circ, 45^\circ, 50^\circ, 55^\circ, 60^\circ, 65^\circ, 70^\circ, 75^\circ, 80^\circ$;
    \item $\delta$: $0^\circ$.
\end{itemize}

According to the definition of BRDF, it is expressed a function of 4 angles ($\theta_i, \phi_i, \theta_r, \phi_r$) in the spherical coordinate system.
Therefore, it is necessary to convert the CIE standard angles ($\alpha,  \beta, \gamma, \delta$) into the corresponding spherical angles~\cite{2023_Boucher}:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:cie-to-sph}
    \begin{array}{lll}
        \theta_i & = & \gamma                  \\
        \phi_i   & = & \delta -\beta  \ [2\pi] \\
        \theta_r & = & \pi / 2 - \alpha        \\
        \phi_r   & = & \delta + \pi \ [2\pi]
    \end{array}
\end{equation}
Accordingly, the spherical angles $\theta_i, \phi_i, \theta_r, \phi_r$ are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\theta_i$ (16 values): $0^\circ, 10^\circ, 15^\circ, 20^\circ, 25^\circ, 30^\circ, 35^\circ, 40^\circ, 45^\circ, 50^\circ, 55^\circ, 60^\circ, 65^\circ, 70^\circ, 75^\circ, 80^\circ$;
    \item $\phi_i$ (1 value): $360^\circ, 350^\circ, 330^\circ, 310^\circ, 290^\circ, 270^\circ, 250^\circ, 230^\circ, 210^\circ, 200^\circ, 195^\circ, 190^\circ$;
    \item $\theta_r$ (9 values): ranging from $35^\circ$ to $75^\circ$ with a step of $5^\circ$
    \item $\phi_r$ (12 values): $180^\circ$
\end{itemize}
This results in a total of $1728$ ($= 16 \times 1 \times 9 \times 12$) measurements configurations for each samples.
Assuming that all samples are isotropic, the angles $\phi_i$ and $\phi_r$ can be interchanged, allowing observation of the BRDF distribution over the hemispherical space for a given incident direction $(\theta_i, \phi_i)$.
Owing to the symmetric emission pattern of the halogen lamp, the BRDF at the symmetric angle of $\phi_r$ an be obtained from that at $\phi_r$.
Thus the new spherical angles $\theta_i, \phi_i, \theta_r, \phi_r$ for measurements becomes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\theta_i$ (16 values): $0^\circ, 10^\circ, 15^\circ, 20^\circ, 25^\circ, 30^\circ, 35^\circ, 40^\circ, 45^\circ, 50^\circ, 55^\circ, 60^\circ, 65^\circ, 70^\circ, 75^\circ, 80^\circ$;
    \item $\phi_i$ (1 value): $180^\circ$;
    \item $\theta_r$ (9 values): ranging from $35^\circ$ to $75^\circ$ with a step of $5^\circ$
    \item $\phi_r$ (24 values): $0^\circ, 10^\circ, 30^\circ, 50^\circ, 70^\circ, 90^\circ, 110^\circ, 130^\circ, 150^\circ, 160^\circ, 165^\circ, 170^\circ$, $190^\circ, 195^\circ$, $200^\circ, 210^\circ, 230^\circ, 250^\circ, 270^\circ, 290^\circ, 310^\circ, 330^\circ, 360^\circ$
\end{itemize}
In reality, although only $1728$ measurements are required, this process yields $3456$ measured data points.
As know, the BRDF represents the ratio of reflected radiance to incident irradiance, requiring both quantities to be measured separately.
The incident irradiance at normal incidence ($\theta_i = 0^\circ$) over the wavelength range of $350 nm$ to $2500 nm$, is first measured using a calibrated diffuser.
For oblique incident angles, the corresponding irradiance estimated by scaling the normal-incident irradiance by the cosine of the incidence angle $\cos\theta_i$.
This cosine dependence is experimentally verified by comparing the measured flux at various incidence angles $\theta_i$ with the predicted flux, calculated as the normal-incident flux $F_{\theta_i=0}$ multiplied by $\cos\theta_i$.
The comparison is shown in Table~\ref{tab:measured_flux_comparison}.
Notice that the relative error is the ratio between the absolute difference of the measured and predicted fluxes and the measured flux.
With $\theta_i$ ranging from $0^\circ$ to $80^\circ$, the relative deviation remains below $5\%$, confirming the validity of the cosine law.
When $\theta_i$ exceeds $80^\circ$, the light source becomes nearly coplanar with the sample surface.
Due to the conical emission profile of the light source (Halogen lamp), the measured flux does not vanish as $\theta_i$ approaches $90^\circ$.
For this reason, measurements at incident angles greater than than $80^\circ$ were excluded to maintain measurement reliability.
After obtaining the incident irradiance, the focus shifts to the measurements of the reflected radiance for all selected samples.

In order to obtain accurate and reliable measurements of spectral radiance(step: 1-2-3-4-6-7-8) or irradiance (step: 1-2-3-4-5-7-8), the procedure is as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Turn on the halogen lamp and let it warm up $25$ minutes to generate stable radiation;
    \item Turn on the goniometer,the computer connected it and the software controlling it;
    \item Turn on the spectroradiometer, the computer connected it and the software used to record the spectral irradiance or radiance;
    \item Verify good connection between the lens and spectroradiometer;
    \item When measuring irradiance, place the diffuser at the center of the sample holder and adjust the height of the sample holder, ensuring that its surface is aligned horizontally with both the halogen lamp at $\theta_i = 90^\circ$ and the camera at $\theta_r = 90^\circ$;
    \item When measuring radiance,
          \begin{itemize}
              \item place the test sample on the center of the sample holder and adjust the height of sample holder,  ensuring that its surface is aligned horizontally with both the halogen lamp at $\theta_i = 90^\circ$ and the camera at $\theta_r = 90^\circ$;
              \item adjust the position of the lens, ensuring it is aligned vertically with both camera and the sample holder;
              \item adjust the height of the lens to correspond to the given configuration angle $\theta_r$;
          \end{itemize}
    \item Launch the codes to start the measurements for all given angles configurations ($\theta_i,\phi_i, \theta_r, \phi_r$);
    \item Let the software automatically record the irradiance or radiance;
\end{enumerate}
Having both measured irradiance and radiance, we can compute the spectral BRDF for each sample.

\subsection{Validation on a calibrated reflectance target}

The first validation is to compare the measured BRDF of spectralon with its theoretical BRDF.
The Spectralon is a white surface which has near-perfect Lambertian behavior.
For a given diffuse albedo $\rho_d$, its BRDF can be computed as:
\begin{equation}
    \label{eq:lambertian}
    f_r(\theta_i, \phi_i, \theta_r, \phi_r, \lambda) = \frac{\rho_d}{\pi}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:brdf-spectralon} shows the measured BRDF of Spectralon with a diffuse albedo of $0.5$ at incident normal direction from $350$ to $2500 nm$, considering different observation angles.
The measured BRDF exhibits noticeable fluctuations along with wavelength, particularly in the near-infrared region.
To better understand how far from measured data to the expected BRDF of Spectralon which is $\rho/\pi=0.16$, the relative errors at each wavelength are calculated and displayed in Figure~\ref{fig:rel-err-spectralon}.
In the visible range, relative errors remain below $20\%$, while in the near-infrared region they are generally below $60\%$, with larger variations observed.
Overall, the results show consistency with the expected behavior between $500nm$ and $1725nm$, confirming the accuracy and reliability of the proposed measurement method.

\subsection{Measured spectral BRDF of 5 samples from $350$–$2500nm$}

For each measurement configuration characterized by the spherical angles $\theta_i, \phi_i, \theta_r, \phi_r$, the spectral BRDF values are determined over the wavelength range from $350nm$ to $2500nm$.
Figure~\ref{fig:all-brdf-wavelength} illustrates the measured BRDF for a particular incident direction ($\theta_i=60^\circ, \phi_i=180^\circ$) under both forward ($\theta_r=60^\circ, \phi_r=0^\circ$) and backward ($\theta_r=60^\circ, \phi_r=165^\circ$) observation geometries.
Across all samples, the BRDF values exhibit a monotonic increase within the visible range, followed by pronounced oscillations in the infrared region.
Notably, in the forward direction, the BRDF values of F2, mastic F2, and mastic F3 are consistently higher than in the backward direction, whereas F3 and Lazard 6/10 display the opposite trend. Lazard 2/6 shows only minor directional dependence.
Comparison across samples reveals that single-component Lazard samples exhibit higher BRDF values than F2 and F3 composites in both the visible and infrared ranges, irrespective of observation direction.
In the backward direction, mastic F3 and mastic F2 have lower BRDF values than their respective base materials (F3 and F2), while in the forward direction, the opposite trend is observed. 
This effect is particularly pronounced for mastic F3, which shows a substantial forward–backward asymmetry, indicative of strong specular reflection behavior.

\subsection{Impact of binder}

As mentioned previously, F2 is composed of lazard 2/6, lazard 6/10 and mastic F2, while F3 consists of the same aggregates combined with mastic F3.
To better understand the relationship between the BRDF values of F2 and F3 and those of their individual components,a cross-sectional cut of the BRDF distribution at $\theta_r = 60^\circ$ for each sample was extracted, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:brdf-distribution-polar}.
For both F2 and F3, the BRDF values remain below those of their components except at the specular direction.
This observation motivates an investigation into whether the BRDF of F2 or F3 can be represented as a linear or nonlinear combination of the BRDFs of their constituent components.

In both the full mixtures and the corresponding mastics, the only differing component is the binder: F2 and mastic F2 contain a synthetic binder, whereas F3 and mastic F3 use bitumen.
As seen in Figure~\ref{fig:brdf-comparison-binder}, both F2 and mastic F2 has lower BRDF values comparing to F3 and mastic F3, respectively.
This consistent difference indicates that the binder has a significant contribution in governing the reflective behavior of multi-component road materials. 
The lower BRDF values observed in bitumen-based mixtures suggest that bitumen reduced surface reflectivity, likely due to its dark color and so low reflectivity property.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec: conclusion}


Both validations using spectralon with albedo of $0.5$ and comparing measured data to the ones measured by UGE, prove that the proposed spectral BRDF measurement approached shows a good accuracy.
For the study of spectral BRDF of roads surface, we conduct many measurements on the multi-material roads and their components.
The data demonstrates that the binder has a significant contribution on the BRDF behavior of the mixture roads.
Besides, the aggregates with different size has few difference except at the specular direction, which may have different contribution on the BRDF of mixture at the specular direction.



In future work, the measured spectral BRDF data can be used to calibrate and evaluate existing reflectance models, such as the Cook Torrance model~\cite{1982_Cook}, the Oren Nayar model~\cite{1995_Oren}, and the Ashikhmin Shirley model~\cite{2000_Ashikhmin}. 
Furthermore, it will be of interest to develop a hybrid model that combines the most appropriate BRDF models for each material component to represent the BRDF of composite road surfaces.
This can provide a foundation for predicting the spectral BRDF of more complex pavement materials.
